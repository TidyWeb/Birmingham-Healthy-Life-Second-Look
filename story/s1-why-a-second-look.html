Chapter S1

Why a second look

I want the claims to be as strong as the evidence: no stronger, and no weaker.

In short

This is my Unit 3 rebuild of Birmingham Healthy Life, seen through an ethical lens. I want to describe what the evidence supports without hiding uncomfortable findings or claiming more than it can show.

What this is

Birmingham Healthy Life began as my Unit 2 project. In Unit 3 I am revisiting that work, examining the choices behind it and rebuilding how I explain the findings. The original chapters remain available as the project I am looking at again.

“The aim is the truth about healthy life expectancy, told plainly. Ethical care means not causing avoidable harm and not claiming more than the evidence shows. It does not mean avoiding uncomfortable findings.”

Respect without avoiding the question

Respecting people and trying to avoid harm matter. That is different from becoming so worried about the possibility of offence that I stop saying what the evidence shows. Trying to please everyone can leave the account too vague to be useful.

The message and the way each reader receives it are not the same thing. I cannot control every interpretation. I am responsible for the claim I make and how strongly I make it.

An accuracy test

A claim should be exactly as strong as its evidence. Overclaiming is an accuracy failure: a precise ranking may not be supported when estimates overlap, and an association does not show a cause. Underclaiming is also an accuracy failure. If a real inequality is visible, softening it until it disappears would conceal something residents have a reason to see.

The figures are estimates, with margins of error. That is a reason to show uncertainty honestly, not a reason to stay silent. A single value can make a map readable; ranges are more appropriate where a ranking or comparison could otherwise imply more certainty than the estimate supports.

Why rank and name places

The investigation is about healthy life expectancy, so I rank areas by healthy life expectancy. I name areas so people who know Birmingham and its surroundings can recognise them and relate the results to places they know.

The datasets were chosen through judgement. I began with measures that seemed plausibly connected to healthy life expectancy, based on intuition and life experience. I left out measures with no plausible route to the question, then plotted the selected data against healthy life expectancy to see what related and what stood out. Those choices and the reasons for keeping or dropping a dataset should be visible rather than left implicit.

Association, not cause; areas, not people

The measures are candidates for association with healthy life expectancy. They are not explanations of why a person is healthy, and the area results are not statements about the individuals who live there. These limits remain central to how I describe the work.

What changes from Unit 2

My Unit 2 ethics account concentrated on privacy. The screen in this second look found that privacy was the part the project largely handled well: all four legal-trigger questions had no flag. It also surfaced questions about interpretation, certainty, possible proxy effects in the model, and accountability to people described by the results. Unit 3 widens the ethical frame to include those issues.
