::: keypoints
In Unit 2 I thought ethics in data meant privacy in relation to individuals, and some of that protection came from fitting my different granularity datasets together. My Signal Box tool made clear to me there were harms I wasn’t looking for, stigma above all. I’ve decided that risk is bearable because I describe and don’t judge, and I’m honest that the benefit is mainly mine. The data didn’t change; what changed is how carefully I say things, and who I’m saying them to.
:::

When I started Unit 2, my idea of ethics in data was privacy. I was working with health data, and when you're dealing with people's health, protecting personal information is the first thing you think about. So I looked at it closely. The healthy life expectancy figures came at the level of Middle Layer Super Output Areas, neighbourhoods of between five and fifteen thousand people, so I went and educated myself about what MSOA meant. It was about dataframe granularity. Nobody could be picked out of an area that size. Some of my other data came at a finer level, LSOA or Lower Layer Super Output Areas. I converted all of it to match. I took comfort in that. As far as I was concerned, there was no way on earth anyone could be identified, and that was the sum total of my ethical thinking.

I have to be honest about that conversion, though. I didn't do it to protect anyone. I did it because healthy life expectancy was my master data frame, and everything else had to fit it so the maps and charts had something consistent to show. The privacy protection was a side benefit. I noticed it and I was glad of it, but it wasn't a decision. Looking back, that's the heart of what Unit 3 taught me: the one protection I had in Unit 2 arrived almost by accident, so I wasn't looking for any of the others.

My Signal Box tool showed me the others. It's the 27-question ethics check I built for my Ethical Considerations in AI guide, to help other students with the Code Institute ethics lesson, and Unit 3 is the first time I've turned it on a real project of my own. It raised seventeen concerns I'd never written down. They weren't the kind of direct threat that identifying an individual person is. They were more abstract than that: consequences, knock-on effects, things that happen after the data is published rather than inside it. The rankings looked more certain than they were. The measures in my model could stand in for an area's ethnic make-up. Nobody had any way to tell me I'd got something wrong. And above all of them, there was stigma.

I hadn't thought about stigma at all in Unit 2. Now I've thought about it a lot. When you publish a map that ranks named places by how many years of good health people can expect, the places at the bottom carry that label, and the people who live there didn't ask for it. My own tool made me face that squarely. When I re-tested my answers with the stricter test I'd added, where I have to be able to say out loud what could go wrong, who it would happen to, what deals with it and who's responsible, fourteen passed and one didn't. That was Q4: The benefit of this project is mostly mine, and if anyone pays a cost through stigma, it's the residents of the areas at the bottom, who had no say. That lamp went back to orange, and it was the most useful thing the tool did.

So I had to decide, and I've made an executive decision that the risk/cost is bearable. I come to data analysis to uncover some truth about a real situation, and I accept that I can't please all the people all the time. The truth about healthy life expectancy in my area and the areas around it matters more to me than the risk of someone feeling labelled by it. I am comfortable sayihng that and the reason is, I describe; I don't judge. I'm not naming and shaming anywhere. I'm not saying don't live here, and I'm not saying anything about the people. More importantly, I don't create the conditions either. House prices and insurance quotes in those areas already reflect the reality there. I come afterwards and look at what is.  My project does not create an alternate reality or narrative.

What could create a narrative is a careless label, and that's where the care has gone. When I simulated the rankings ten thousand times, only 35 of the 66 lowest areas stayed in the bottom tenth in at least 95 out of every hundred runs. The other 31 can't honestly be called among the lowest. In Unit 2, they would have been. So the site now says how sure each label is, in plain words: clearly, probably or possibly among the lowest. I used simple natural language in my labels  for people to read, not a statistically definitive file of code for machines to parse.

I've also had to be honest about who benefits. Cui bono? It's me. This is a student project, a demonstration of technique, and I made it for an assessment and a portfolio. It's public, though, and I've sent it to some senior NHS professionals I know for their view on whether the dashboard is clear - the logic sound. So it may reach people who can act on it, or it may not. I've accepted that the cost may fall on others while the benefit is mainly mine, and I've said so openly on the site.

My guiding star throughout this has been the differences. I went looking for big gaps between areas that sit right next to each other, because they're clear and visually powerful, and that raises a question. Why should people in one neighbourhood expect many years more good health than people in the neighbourhood next door? I'm not saying I've answered that. I'm saying I've raised it as a question. This work is stage one of an inquiry, not the end of one. Looking back, Unit 2 presented those gaps more like answers than questions, with exact ranks and single figures. Unit 3 is where the presentation caught up with what I meant.

The practical side had its own lessons. Some of my mistakes only showed when I checked the numbers against the output: an early count of sixteen concerns that should have been seventeen, and a rule for spotting mixed areas that compared two things that weren't alike and flagged 581 areas instead of 255. A big number from a bad rule sounds like a strong finding, and that's exactly why it's dangerous. Working with AI tools was fast, but it needed a firm hand. An email went to Code Institute that I hadn't approved, work got scattered across copies of the same repository, and I learned to set rules for the tools before they caused problems rather than after. Five people (including myself) then tested the site and rated every task green, which confirmed to me the dashboard and its explanations work for someone who isn't me.

Would I do Unit 2 differently? Yes. The ethics-first lens didn't change the data, and it hasn't changed what I visualised. The questions are as valid now as they were then. What it changed is how careful I am: about jumping to conclusions, and about drawing black-and-white distinctions where they don't exist. And it changed something I didn't expect, which is my sense of who I'm speaking to. I'd spent my time building the most thorough, technical project I could, scatter plots and statistical methods one after another, so I could pass my assessments and show employers what I can do. To a non-technical reader, that's a blizzard of data. My tutor, Vasi, put it simply: a senior manager should be able to ask the dashboard a simple question and get a simple answer. Mine couldn't. Now it opens with plain questions and plain answers, and the technical detail is one click away for anyone who wants it.

I looked at creating a natural language AI chat-bot, but considered it beyond the scope of the task in hand, but if I were to build such a data dashboard again for an actual client, I would build it.  Simple natural language query of complex data is I think the future.

So in the end, the data didn't change, and the questions didn't change. What changed is how carefully I say things, and how I say them, depending upon who I'm saying them to.

That is a valuable lesson.
