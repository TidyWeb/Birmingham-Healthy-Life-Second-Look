I converted source tables, counts and grid values into comparable measures for the same areas. The screen's concern is that area averages can hide thin groups and within-area differences.
:::

## What I did in Unit 2

^ nb02#0ea58e59 | The prepared data cover the same 660 study areas.
I selected the study areas, checked fields and missing values, then saved compact tables and read them back. I summed fuel-poor household counts from smaller areas before calculating rates, weighted pollution grid cells by how much of each cell fell inside an area, and divided recorded crime counts by resident population. I kept the source files unchanged and flagged unusual areas rather than deleting them.

## What the screen asked

**Q10 — Are groups missing or thin? Concern.** The screen found that detailed ethnic groups can be very small and that MSOA averages hide variation inside an area. It also recorded six student-dominated areas and six visitor hubs as unusual cases; I kept them and marked the limits of how some measures describe them.

**Cleared — Q5, information about identifiable people? No flag.** The published outputs are area aggregates. Police incidents are rolled up to rates and are not published at point level.

**Cleared — Q6, profiling individuals? No flag.** Both models estimate area values, not personal outcomes.

## What I found

The preparation kept the flagged areas in the analysis and kept the unit of analysis at area level. A check of variation inside each area has not been run here; that question is covered later in this second look.

## What changes

I retain the area aggregates and state what they leave out. Any later small-group or within-area result must keep its own unit and limits clear; area estimates are not statements about individual residents.
